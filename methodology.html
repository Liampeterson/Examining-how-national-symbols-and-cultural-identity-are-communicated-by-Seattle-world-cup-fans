Methodology

The Methods Informing our Project

Based in Seattle, twenty-one undergraduate researchers in the Summer Institute for Arts of Humanities (SIAH) at UW completed fieldwork collaboratively and independently across official FIFA zones, city specified fanzones and watch parties from June 19th-July 19th during the 2026 FIFA Men’s World Cup. The 2026 SIAH teaching team directed participants in utilizing a mixed-method approach to the program’s theme, "Seattle's World Cup: Storytelling Through Community Mapping." The bulk of the fieldwork was completed in small groups during the group stage and knock-out stage of the global tournament while Seattle was an active FIFA host city. Lumen Field, disguised as Seattle Stadium, was the site for six tournament fixtures and of those six, four are highlighted below where SIAH participants worked on site. For matches not hosted in Seattle, official and unofficial watch parties were held throughout the host city. Contributors all reside within the Seattle Metro area, thus some also completed fieldwork within the regions we had selected to show the most outward, in person participation and performance of football fandom. This case study uses a thematic narrative analysis and considers key factors and material symbols of fandom; costumery, ritual, flags and jerseys. Data collection was up close and personal, and our representations of fandom is further personalized through our mostly-manual analysis involving symbolism and behaviour; noting the shirt on someone’s back, the paint on their face, the flag they hold waving in the wind, their movement in a sportful ceremony. With contributors’ fields of studies and backgrounds differing, we believe to be at an advantage to broaden interpretations of national fandom and to meet wider audiences in doing so. Utilizing and expanding upon existing critical sport studies and broader social sciences research and frameworks, our process was developed interdisciplinarily. Processing methods were chosen based upon the authors’ belief that any attempt to analyze the experience of the fan without grounding the inquiry in the appropriate context of the human experience can do little more than provide a rudimentary understanding of the phenomenon. Conducting an analysis rooted in theory from cultural, sociological, and anthropological frameworks has been emphasized to allow for a greater and more nuanced understanding of both the experience of the human as a fan and the fan as a human. Frameworks such as dramaturgy, sport performativity theory and symbolic interactionism were determined to be useful in examining how fans dance with nationality and cultural identity in dress and behavior and coexist with others in federation football nexuses. In presenting our works to wider general and academic audiences, we have sought to feature authentic fan experiences rather than totalities of national identities.

A visual and sensory ethnographic framework is deployed, in which photography and videography serve as an autonomous research method rather than one primarily supplement to text. We reject the notion that realism or objective neutrality defines documentary work; and see visual ethnography as inherently both expressive and documentary. Researchers have attempted to maintain reflexivity and critique themselves in their own encounters rather than treating the field as an exotic location as done in traditional ethnography. With the major events of interest taking place in the city in which the researchers live and study, efforts have been bolstered by deeper understandings of the host city’s strengths and needs. Continuing education in critical sports studies and receiving mentorship from SIAH’s teaching team has been key in preparing our works to the public.

As the 2026 tournament and classwide fieldwork neared its end, SIAH participants were structured in small groups with shared research interests and led to produce a final project and symposium held at UW on August 21st, 2026. With individual interest in furthering studies in national fan identity and/or multiculturalism during and beyond the blurred temporal and spatial bounds of “The World Cup,” we formed to develop an interdisciplinary research process and to communicate our findings creatively and contextually. Featuring four individual photo essays hosted on an interactive, custom website, a digital space of our witnessing and wondering, our study and interpretation, has been built to encourage critical fandom inquiry and understanding through means suspected to be more easily accessible than a typical academic article.

Data Collection Methods

Observational Research, Participant Observation, Digital photography and videography, Action Mapping Project (AMP) survey collection, Relationship building, Participatory Interviews

Data Processing Methods and Tools

University of Washington Libraries, Manual Inspection of text and media, Adobe Creative Suite, ImageJ, Group Discussions, Researcher Reflexivity, Thematic Coding

Methods of Delivery

Photo Essays, Website (GitHub repository), Symposium, Documentary and Expressive storytelling

Analysis

Our analysis of the "fan experience" is inherently informed by our experience as a participant in the exercise of fandom. Therefore, instead of using our research as a means of conducting an empirical experiment, these four photo essays are curated to explore four key areas of fan identity, each using selected theoretical frameworks and individual methods to ground their respective analyses.
