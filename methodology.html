Methodology

How Did We Get Here?

In Seattle, Washington, twenty undergraduate researchers participating in the 2026 Summer Institute for Arts of Humanities (SIAH) completed fieldwork both collaboratively and independently across official FIFA zones, city specified fanzones and watch parties from June 19th-July 19th during the 2026 FIFA Men’s World Cup. The 2026 SIAH teaching team directed participants in utilizing a mixed-method approach to the program’s theme, "Seattle's World Cup: Storytelling Through Community Mapping." The bulk of the fieldwork was completed in small groups during the group stage and knock-out stage of the global tournament while Seattle was an active FIFA host city. Lumen Field, disguised as Seattle Stadium, was the site for six tournament fixtures and of those six, four are highlighted below where SIAH participants worked on site. For matches not hosted in Seattle, official and unofficial watch parties were held throughout the host city. Contributors all reside within the Seattle Metro area, thus some also completed fieldwork within the regions we had selected to show the most outward, in person participation and performance of football fandom.

As the 2026 tournament and classwide fieldwork neared its end, SIAH participants were structured in small groups with shared research interests and led to produce a final project and symposium held at UW on August 21st, 2026. With individual interest in furthering studies in national fan identity and/or multiculturalism during and beyond the blurred temporal and spatial bounds of “The World Cup,” we formed to develop an interdisciplinary research process and to communicate our findings creatively and contextually. This case study uses a thematic narrative analysis and primarily considers the following key factors and material symbols of fandom:

  • Costumery — the world’s biggest sporting event brings with it beautifully expressive displays of national representation and pride, as many fans compete to show off the most extravagant displays of their culture.
  • Ritual — in interactions with countrymen, opposition, and neutrals alike, fan behavior and expression on gameday is shaped by generations of football fans before us.
  • Flags — a symbol so deeply tied to the history and context of a nation, waving your favorite team’s flag on match day expresses much more than simple love for team or country.
  • Jerseys — the nearly endless options for the default matchday uniform provide a ripe opportunity for displays of identity and passion.

Noting the shirt on someone’s back, the paint on their face, the flag they hold waving in the wind, their movement in a sportful ceremony; data collection was up close and personal, and our representation of fandom was further personalized through our mostly-manual inspection. Additionally, with contributors’ fields of studies and backgrounds differing, we believe to be at an advantage to broaden interpretations of national fandom and to meet wider audiences in doing so. Utilizing and expanding upon existing critical sport studies and broader social sciences research and frameworks, our process was developed interdisciplinarily. Processing methods were chosen based upon the authors’ belief that any attempt to analyze the experience of the fan without grounding the inquiry in the appropriate context of the human experience can do little more than provide a rudimentary understanding of the phenomenon. Conducting an analysis rooted in theory from cultural, sociological, and anthropological frameworks has been emphasized to allow for a greater and more nuanced understanding of both the experience of the human as a fan and the fan as a human. Frameworks such as sport performativity theory and symbolic interactionism that were determined to be useful in examining how fans dance with national and cultural identity in dress and behavior and coexist with others in federation football nexuses. A recognition for the more-than-human is brought through relational epistemologies applied to the research process as well, in which artificial dichotomies and essentialist thought of fandom have been deconstructed, favoring a view of fan identity as a lived experience rather than a classifying function. In presenting our work to both general and academic audiences, we especially have sought to feature authentic fan occurrences in Seattle rather than totalities of national identities.

A visual and sensory ethnographic framework has been deployed in which photography serves as an autonomous research method instead of one primarily supplement to text. We reject the notion that realism or objective neutrality defines documentary work; and see visual ethnography as inherently both expressive and documentary. Digital photographs were created on match-days in the areas of interest, primarily in the Pioneer Square and Stadium District neighborhoods, with group members Sam Hurst and Moriel O’Connor acting as photographers with Canon DSLRS. In participatory observation, researchers have attempted to maintain reflexivity and critique themselves in their own encounters rather than treating the field as an exotic location as done in traditional ethnography. With the matches of interest taking place in the city in which the researchers live and study, efforts have been bolstered by deeper understandings of the host city’s strengths and needs in welcoming and sustaining fandoms. Continuing education in critical sports studies with the wider SIAH cohort, receiving mentorship from SIAH’s teaching team, Caley Cook, Ron Krabill and Yasir Zaidan has been key in preparing our works to the public. Featuring four individual photo essays hosted on a custom website; glimpses of our witnessing and wondering, our study and interpretation, have been archived to encourage critical fandom inquiry and reflection. Suspected to be more easily accessible than a typical academic article or empirical study, users are encouraged to engage with the digital space at their own pace, are welcomed to return, and are able to request access to the digital materials gathered for purposes of story-telling and education.

Data Processing Methods and Tools

  • Thematic Coding — Centering our analysis of fan identity around four key categories of interest: flags, jerseys, costumes, and ritual.
  • Manual Inspection of text and media — Examining academic sources, digital media, and photography relevant to our research question.
  • Adobe Creative Suite — Used to edit our photos.
  • Group Discussions — Used for guiding our approach to exploring the medium, analysis, and final product design.
  • Researcher Reflexivity — Direct engagement with each researcher’s own experiences as both a fan and scholar, shaping fieldwork and analysis.

Analysis

Our analysis of the "fan experience" is inherently informed by our experience as a participant in the exercise of fandom. Therefore, instead of using our research as a means of conducting an empirical experiment, these four photo essays are curated to explore four key areas of fan identity, each using selected theoretical frameworks and individual methods to ground their respective analyses.
