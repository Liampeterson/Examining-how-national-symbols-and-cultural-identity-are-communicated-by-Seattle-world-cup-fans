Introduction

Before the first whistle blows in Seattle Stadium, the energy on the street is already electric. Flags fly proudly along the waterfront, jerseys that have waited patiently in closets finally see daylight, and wild costumes spark conversations, turning strangers into a sudden community. It’s been nearly thirty years since the World Cup last touched American soil, and with every match comes another chance for the global community to bring their diverse life experiences and perspectives to our amazing city.

While most eyes stayed fixed on the game, ours stayed fixed on the people who make it mean something: the fans. In a way, fandom works like a wave that ripples through a stadium: each section rises on its own, and in its own moment, in its own style, no two arms lifted quite the same way. Alone, any one section is a scatter of people standing up and sitting down, but strung together, the same gesture becomes something bigger than any single fan could make happen alone. What we found is that fandom isn’t one fixed thing, but something that shifts with context, with audience, with who’s standing next to you. By looking closely at these elements and reading them against the frameworks the scholars have built to understand fan identity, we hope to understand not just what fans do, but why fandom looks different where you are.

Our result is a series of photo essays telling the story of Seattle’s fan experience during the 2026 FIFA World Cup, with analysis grounded equally in lived observation and in the ideas that help make sense of it. Every photo really does speak a thousand worlds, and behind each one sits a question we were trying to answer. We focused our lens on four symbolic attributes that give a window into something larger: flags, jerseys, costumery, and rituals.

However, observation can only take a story so far. Alongside our field notes and photographs, we turned to scholarship about fandom and identity. These frames help explain why a flag draped over someone’s shoulder means something different at a bar than it does on a sidewalk, or why a chant is passed from parent to child, carrying the weight of ritual and belonging rather than just pure noise. These theories don’t sit over our photographs as some detached academic layer; rather, we tried to let them move through the images themselves, giving language to what we were seeing on the ground.

F-01

Flags

Flags convert private loyalty into a public, legible claim — visible across a stadium concourse or a living room window long before anyone speaks. They mark territory and allegiance at a glance, making them one of the most immediate symbols fans use to announce who they support and where they belong.

J-01

Jerseys

Jerseys function as a wearable, everyday extension of identity — a second skin layered with names, numbers, and club or national crests. Because they're worn on ordinary days as much as match days, jerseys blur the line between spectator and belonging, turning identity into something lived rather than performed only in the moment.

R-01

Rituals

Chants, songs, and repeated fan traditions synchronize individual bodies into a single collective voice. These rituals matter because they turn isolated spectators into a coordinated group, reinforcing shared memory and belonging each time the same words and gestures are repeated together.

C-01

Costumes

Face paint, wigs, and elaborate dress push everyday fandom into deliberate performance and spectacle. Costumes matter here because they show identity being consciously staged, a fan choosing, often with real time and effort, exactly how their allegiance should be seen.
